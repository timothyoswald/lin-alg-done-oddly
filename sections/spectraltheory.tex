\oldsection{Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors}
Here we will only consider linear transformations $T_A:V\to V$ via $T_A(\vec{x})=A\vec{x}$ where $V$ is an arbitrary vector space over some field $\mathbb{F}$. In general, it is most common for this vector space to be $\mathbb{C}^n$ and we'll discuss the algebraic motivation behind this shortly. The motivation behind this chapter is the fact that often times we need to repeatedly apply linear transformations. This means that we need to compute $A^n\vec{x}$, but this is an expensive computation since matrix multiplication is not trivial to compute. In this chapter, we will analyze when we have "nicer" ways to compute $A^n\vec{x}$ and to what extent we can determine when we are in these scenarios.
\begin{definition}
    Let $A$ be a $n\times n$ matrix. Assume that we have $A\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$ for $\vec{x}\in V\setminus\{\vec{0}\}$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$. We call $\lambda$ an eigenvalue of $A$ and $\vec{x}$ a corresponding eigenvector.
\end{definition}
Let's analyze when we have a solution for $A\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}$.
\begin{align*}
    A\vec{x}&=\lambda\vec{x}\\
    A\vec{x}-\lambda\vec{x}&=\vec{0}\\
    A\vec{x}-\lambda I\vec{x}&=\vec{0}\\
    (A-\lambda I)\vec{x}&=\vec{0}
\end{align*}
If $A-\lambda I$ is invertible, then this equation has exactly one solution: the zero vector. We require that eigenvectors are non-zero so we care about the solutions when $A-\lambda I$ is not invertible. As we know, this is equation has nontrivial solutions exactly when $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.
\begin{example}
    Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}
        2 & 1 \\
        1 & 2
    \end{bmatrix}$. We have that $A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}
        2 - \lambda & 1\\
        1 & 2 - \lambda
    \end{bmatrix}$ and $\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1$.
    \begin{align*}
        (2-\lambda)^2-1&=0\\
        4-4\lambda+\lambda^2-1&=0\\
        \lambda^2-4\lambda+3&=0\\
        (\lambda -3)(\lambda-1)&=0
    \end{align*}
    This tells us that $1$ and $3$ are eigenvalues for $A$. Now, let's find their corresponding eigenvectors. First, let $\lambda=1$.
    \begin{align*}
        A\vec{x}&=\lambda\vec{x}\\
        A\vec{x}&=\vec{x}\\
        \begin{bmatrix}
            2 & 1\\
            1 & 2
        \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
            x_1 \\ x_2
        \end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
            x_1 \\ x_2
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{align*}
    This gives us the equations $2x_1+x_2=x_1$ and $x_1+2x_2=x_2$. These tell us that $x_1=-x_2$. Moreover, any vector that satisfies $\begin{pmatrix}
        a \\ -a
    \end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $1$.

    Now let $\lambda=3$. We perform a similar analysis.
    \begin{align*}
        A\vec{x}&=\lambda\vec{x}\\
        A\vec{x}&=3\vec{x}\\
        \begin{bmatrix}
            2 & 1\\
            1 & 2
        \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
            x_1 \\ x_2
        \end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
            3x_1 \\ 3x_2
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{align*}
    This gives us the equations $2x_1+x_2=3x_1$ and $x_1+2x_2=3x_2$. Solving this system gives us that $x_1=x_2$. So the eigenvectors with eigenvalue $3$ are any vector that satisfy $\begin{pmatrix}
        a \\ a
    \end{pmatrix}$.
\end{example}
The previous example actually tells us something more about eigenvectors. We notice that when we solved the system, we got eigenvectors of a certain form not just one specific vector. In fact, once you have one eigenvector, you can generate many more by scaling.
\begin{example}
    Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0\\
        0 & 0 & 0\\
       0 & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}$. We can compute that $\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2(1-\lambda)$ which has roots $1$ and $0$. Let's consider the case where $\lambda =0$. We want to solve $A\vec{x}=0\vec{x}=\vec{0}$.
    \begin{align*}
        A\vec{x}&=\vec{0}\\
        \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 & 0\\
        0 & 0 & 0\\
       0 & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
        x \\ y \\ z
    \end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
        x \\ 0 \\ 0
    \end{bmatrix}=\vec{0}
    \end{align*}
    This implies that as long as the first coordinate is zero, the other coordinates can be anything. In particular, $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$ are both eigenvectors and every vector in their span is also an eigenvector.

    The case where $\lambda=1$ is uninteresting so we omit it.
\end{example}
From the previous examples, we can see that the term eigenvector is not unique. A singular eigenvalue can have many, often infinitely many eigenvectors. This leads to the following definition:
\begin{definition}
    Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of a matrix $A$. The eigenspace of $\lambda$ is the space spanned by the corresponding eigenvectors. The dimension of the eigenspace for an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is known as the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
    So in the previous example, the eigenspace of $0$ would be $span({(0,1,0),(1,0,0)})$
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
    We discussed above how eigenvalues are exactly the solutions to the equation $(A-\lambda I)\vec{x}=\vec{0}$. This implies that the eigenspace is equivalent to the kernel of the transformation $A-\lambda I$.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
    When finding eigenvalues, we call the equation $\det(A-\lambda I)$ the characteristic polynomial.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}
    Let $T: \mathbb{P}^n\to\mathbb{P}^n$ be the derivative operator. What are the eigenvalues of $T$?

    First, we notice that if the input has degree $k\geq 1$, then the output has degree $k-1$. There is no way to scale a degree $k-1$ polynomial by a constant to get a degree $k$ polynomial thus there are no eigenvalues for polynomials of degree $k\geq 1$.

    This leaves the case when $k<1$. When $k<1$, our polynomial is simply a constant. The derivative of a constant is just $0$. Thus, this tells us that $T$ has eigenvalue $0$ which has eigenvectors that are any constant.
\end{example}
Before further discussion, we need to briefly discuss some algebra as solving the characteristic polynomial is the key component of finding eigenvalues. It is common to work in $\C^n$ when discussing eigenvalues because polynomials always have roots in the complex numbers. In particular, a polynomial of degree $n$ always has $n$ roots (counting multiplicities) over the complex numbers. Counting multiplicities means that if a polynomial is divisible by $(x-3)^5$, we count $3$ as a root $5$ times.
\begin{definition}
    The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ for a matrix $A$ is its degree in the characteristic polynomial of $A$.
\end{definition}
This is important because when we analyze the characteristic polynomial of an $n\times n$ matrix $A$, $n$ $\lambda$ terms along the main diagonal of $A-\lambda I$. When we compute $\det(A-\lambda I)$, we will end up with a degree $n$ polynomial whose roots are our eigenvalues.
\begin{theorem}
    Every single variable polynomial with complex coefficients and degree at least one has at least one complex root. This is the \textit{Fundamental Theorem of Algebra}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}
    If $p(z)$ is a polynomial with complex coefficients and we have that $p(c)=0$, then $(z-c)$ divides $p(z)$.
\end{theorem}
We will use these as facts without proof as their proofs are highly unrelated to linear algebra. These two facts alone give us a lot of important takeaways.
\begin{corollary}
    Every square matrix $A$ with complex values has at least one eigenvalue.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    If $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix, $\det(A-\lambda I)$ will be a degree $n$ polynomial. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, this has at least one complex root. We know that this root is an eigenvalue so $A$ has at least one eigenvalue.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
    Every degree $n\geq 1$ polynomial has $n$ roots counting multiplicities.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    Let $p(z)$ be our polynomial of degree $n\geq 1$. We induct on $n$. If $n=1$, then the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra gives us what we want. For $n>1$, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra gives us one root $c$. Theorem 5.1.8 tells us that we can divide $p(z)$ by this $z-c$ to get a degree $n-1$ polynomial. Induction now concludes the proof.
\end{proof}

\section{Diagonalization}
We previously discussed taking large powers of $A$. This can be very expensive especially if $A$ is high dimensional. However, if we have the special case where $A$ is diagonal, this computation is really easy since:
$$\begin{bmatrix}
    a & 0 & 0\\
    0 & b & 0\\
    0 & 0 & c
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
    a & 0 & 0\\
    0 & b & 0\\
    0 & 0 & c
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
    a^2 & 0 & 0\\
    0 & b^2 & 0\\
    0 & 0 & c^2
\end{bmatrix}$$
Moreover, by induction we can see that:
$$\begin{bmatrix}
    a & 0 & 0\\
    0 & b & 0\\
    0 & 0 & c
\end{bmatrix}^n=\begin{bmatrix}
    a^n & 0 & 0\\
    0 & b^n & 0\\
    0 & 0 & c^n
\end{bmatrix}$$
\begin{definition}
    $T:V\to V$ is diagonalizable if there exists a basis $B$ for $V$ consisting of eigenvectors of $T$. Let $B=(\vec{v}_1,\ldots,\vec{v}_n)$ with corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ then:
    $$[T]_{BB}=\begin{bmatrix}
        \lambda_1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
        0 & \lambda_2 & 0 & 0\\
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
        0 & 0 & 0 & \lambda_n
    \end{bmatrix}$$
\end{definition}
Let's consider where this matrix comes from. We know that the first column of the matrix is equal to $[T(\vec{v}_1)]_B$. We also know that since $\vec{v}_1$ is an eigenvector, that $T(\vec{v}_1)=\lambda_1\vec{v}_1$. This gives us that $T(\vec{v}_1)=\lambda_1\vec{v}_1+0\vec{v}_2+0\vec{v_3}+\ldots+0\vec{v}_n$. This gives us the coordinate vector shown in the first column of the above matrix. You can apply similar logic to the rest of the columns.

Now, let's talk about how do we find this diagonal matrix. Based on the definition, we could find every single eigenvector and then check if they form a basis. If not, then $T$ is not diagonalizable. Otherwise, let's look at how we get $[T]_{BB}$. If we want, $[T]_{BB}$ we need to use change of basis matrices:
$$[T]_{BB}=[I]_{BE}[T]_{EE}[I]_{EB}$$
Note that $E$ represents the elementary basis and as long as you know the transformation with respect to some basis you can do this. Initially, we only know $[T]_{EE}$.
\begin{align*}
    [T]_{BB}&=[I]_{BE}[T]_{EE}[I]_{EB}\\
    [I]_{BE}^{-1}[T]_{BB}&=[I]_{BE}^{-1}[I]_{BE}[T]_{EE}[I]_{EB}\\
    [I]_{BE}^{-1}[T]_{BB}&=[T]_{EE}[I]_{EB}\\
    [I]_{BE}^{-1}[T]_{BB}[I]_{EB}^{-1}&=[T]_{EE}[I]_{EB}[I]_{EB}^{-1}\\
    [I]_{BE}^{-1}[T]_{BB}[I]_{EB}^{-1}&=[T]_{EE}\\
    [I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}[I]_{BE}&=[T]_{EE}
\end{align*}
A common way this is notated is $P^{-1}DP=A$. Let's talk about why this achieves the efficient power multiplication that want.
\begin{align*}
    ([T]_{EE})^2&=([I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}[I]_{BE})^2\\
    &=([I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}[I]_{BE})([I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}[I]_{BE})\\
    &=([I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}[T]_{BB}[I]_{BE})\\
    &=([I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}^2[I]_{BE})
\end{align*}
By induction, this gives us:
$$([T]_{EE})^n=[I]_{EB}[T]_{BB}^n[I]_{BE}$$
Since $[T]_{BB}$ is diagonal, computing $[T]_{BB}^n$ is simple. Afterwards, we only need to perform two matrix multiplications. This is way more efficient than trying to directly compute $[T]_{EE}^n$ which would require $n$ difficult matrix multiplications.

\section{Minimal Polynomial}
This section will discuss a different point of view on eigenvalues and linear transformations. The idea of a minimal polynomial also manifests itself as the generator of ideals in polynomial rings and we'll see a similar result here.
\begin{definition}
    A monic polynomial is a polynomial where the coefficient of the highest degree term is $1$.
\end{definition}
First, we discuss a slight abuse of notation. Let $p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^nc_ix^i$ be a polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}$. For a linear transformation $\bd{T}:V\to V$ where $V$ is over the field $\mathbb{F}$, we define $p(\bd{T})(\bd{v})=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\bd{T}^i(\bd{v})$.
\begin{theorem}
    Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}$ and $\bd{T}:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Then there exists a unique monic polynomial of smallest degree in $\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F})$ such that $p(\bd{T})=\bd{0}$. Moreover, $\deg(p)\leq\dim(V)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    First, we show existence of such a monic polynomial not necessarily of minimal degree. We proceed by induction on $\dim(V)$. If $\dim(V)=0$, then $V=\{0\}$ and the only linear transformation on $V$ is $\bd{T}(0)=0$. The unique monic polynomial is $p(x)=1$ since then $p(\bd{T})=\text{Id}_V$ but the only vector in $V$ is the zero vector so this is exactly the zero transformation. 

    Now let $\dim(V)>0$ and assume the statement is true for all vector spaces of smaller dimension. Let $n:=\dim(V)$ and $\bd{v}\in V$, $\bd{v}\neq\bd{0}$. Consider the list $(\bd{v},\bd{T}(\bd{v}),\bd{T}^2(\bd{v}),\dots, \bd{T}^n(\bd{v}))$. This is a list of length $n+1$ in an $n$-dimensional space so it must be linearly dependent. This means we can find $k\in[n-1]$ such that $(\bd{v},\bd{T}(\bd{v}),\dots,\bd{T}^k(\bd{v}))$ are linearly independent and $\bd{T}^{k+1}(\bd{v})\in\Span(\{\bd{v},\bd{T}(\bd{v}),\dots,\bd{T}^k(\bd{v})\})$ (can you see why?). Thus, we can write:
    \[c_0\bd{v}+c_1\bd{T}(\bd{v})+\dots+c_k\bd{T}^k(\bd{v})+\bd{T}^{k+1}(\bd{v})=\bd{0}\]
    Let $p(x):=c_0+c_1x+\dots c_kx^k+x^{k+1}$ and note that $p(\bd{T})=0$ since the above holds for any $\bd{v}\in V$. Observe that for any $i\in\N$:
    \[p(\bd{T})(\bd{T}^i(\bd{v}))=T^i(p(T)(\bd{v}))=\bd{T}^i(\bd{0})=\bd{0}\]
    Thus, $\bd{T}^i(\bd{v})\in\ker p(\bd{T})$. In particular, $(\bd{v},\bd{T}(\bd{v}),\bd{T}^2(\bd{v}),\dots, \bd{T}^k(\bd{v}))$ is a linearly independent list in the kernel of $p(\bd{T})$ so $\nll(p(\bd{T}))\geq k+1=\deg(p)$. Using Rank-Nullity, we get that
    \begin{align*}
        \dim(V)-(k+1)\geq\dim(V)-\nll(p(\bd{T}))=\rank(p(\bd{T}))\tag{1}
    \end{align*}

    Consider $\bd{T}:\im(p(\bd{T}))\to \im(p(\bd{T}))$. This is well-defined because for $\bd{y}\in\im(p(\bd{T}))$, there exists some $\bd{x}\in V$ such that $p(\bd{T})(\bd{x})=\bd{y}$:
    \[\bd{T}(\bd{y})=\bd{T}(p(\bd{T})(\bd{x}))=p(\bd{T})(\bd{T}(\bd{x}))\in\im(p(\bd{T}))\]
    (1) tells us $\im(p(\bd{T}))$ has dimension strictly less than $V$. Thus, we can apply the Induction Hypothesis to find a unique monic polynomial of minimal degree $q$ such that $q(\bd{T}\restriction_{\im(p(\bd{T}))})=\bd{0}$ and $\deg(q)\leq\dim(V)-(k+1)$.
    We claim that $qp$ is the desired unique monic polynomial. We compute:
    \[(qp)(\bd{T})(\bd{v})=(q(\bd{T})p(\bd{T}))(\bd{v})=q(\bd{T})(p(\bd{T})(\bd{v}))=\bd{0}\]
    where the final equality follows from the fact that $p(\bd{T})(\bd{v})\in\im(p(\bd{T}))$ so by construction of $q$, $q\restriction_{\im(p(\bd{T}))}=\bd{0}$. Thus, $qp$ is a monic polynomial such that $qp(\bd{T})=\bd{0}$. The degree of polynomials is bounded from below by $0$ thus there exists a minimal one.

    Now we show uniqueness. Let $p,q\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F})$ be two monic polynomials of smallest degrees such that $p(\bd{T})=q(\bd{T})=\bd{0}$ but $p\neq q$. Notice that because $p$ and $q$ are both monic, $\deg(p-q)<\deg(p)=\deg(q)$. Let $c$ be the coefficient of the leading term of $p-q$. Notice that $\frac{1}{c}(p-q)$ is monic and $\frac{1}{c}(p-q)(\bd{T})=\bd{0}$. But this polynomial has smaller degree than $p$ and $q$ so this is a contradiction.
    \end{proof}

    Now that we know such an object exists and is unique, we can formally define it.
    \begin{definition}
        Let $V$ be a finite dimensional vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and $\bd{T}:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Then the \textit{minimal polynomial} of $\bd{T}$ is the unique monic polynomial of smallest degree $p\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F})$ such that $p(\bd{T})=\bd{0}$.
    \end{definition}

Now we can take a look at what relationship this minimal polynomial has to the spectrum of a linear transformation. Note that we will say that a polynomial $p$ \textit{annihilates} a linear transformation $\T$ if $p(\T)=\bd{0}$.

\begin{theorem}
    The set of all roots of the minimal polynomial for a linear transformation $\bd{T}$ is exactly the set of all eigenvalues of $\bd{T}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $p$ be the minimal polynomial of $\bd{T}$.

    \textbf{Step 1:}

    Let $\lambda$ be a root of $p$. Then we can write
    \[
    p(\bd{T})=(\bd{T}-\lambda \bd{I})q(\bd{T})=\bd{0}
    \]
    for some polynomial $q$. Note that $\deg(q)<\deg(p)$. Since $p$ is the minimal polynomial, this means that $q$ cannot annihilate $\bd{T}$ so there must exist a vector $\bd{v}\in V$ such that $q(\bd{T})(\bd{v})\neq\bd{0}$. Now notice that
    \[
    p(\bd{T})(\bd{v})=(\bd{T}-\lambda \bd{I})q(\bd{T})\bd{v}=\bd{0}
    \]
    so $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\bd{T}$ with eigenvector $q(\bd{T})\bd{v}$.

    \textbf{Step 2:}

    Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $\T$ with eigenvector $\vecv$ so $\T(\vecv)=\lambda\vecv$. Write
    \[
    p(\T)=c_0+c_1\T+\dots+c_n\T^n=\bd{0}
    \]
    Using that $\vecv$ is an eigenvector, we see
    \[
    p(\T)\vecv=(c_0+c_1\lambda+\dots+c_n\lambda^n)\vecv=\bd{0}
    \]
    Since $\vecv$ is an eigenvector, it cannot be the zero vector. Thus, we must have that $p(\lambda)=c_0+c_1\lambda+\dots+c_n\lambda^n=0$ so $\lambda$ is a root of $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    The minimal polynomial divides all polynomials which annihilate $\T$. 
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Let $p$ be the minimal polynomial of $\T$ and $q$ be a polynomial which annihilates $\T$. Using the same argument as Step 2 from Theorem 6.1.4, we can see that all eigenvalues of $\T$ are roots of $q$. But the eigenvalues are exactly the roots of $p$ hence $p$ divides $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space and $\T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation such that $\T$ can be written as an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Then:
    $$(\T-\lambda_1 \I)\dots(\T-\lambda_n\I)=\bd{0}$$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We proceed by induction on $n\in\N$. If $n=1$, then $(\T-\lambda_1\I)\vecv_1=\T(\vecv_1)-\lambda_1\vecv_1=\bd{0}$. Assume the statement is true for all $(n-1)$-dimensional spaces so,
    
    $$(\T-\lambda_1 \I)\dots(\T-\lambda_{n-1}\I)=0$$
    
    Let $(\bd{v}_1,\dots,\bd{v}_n)$ be a basis by which $\T$ is upper triangular. In particular the above expression sends $(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{n-1})$ to $\bd{0}$. Now we consider $(\T-\lambda_1 \I)\dots(\T-\lambda_{n-1}\I)(\T-\lambda_n\I)$. Notice that $(\T-\lambda_n\I)\vecv_n\in\Span(\{\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{n-1}\})$. By Induction, $(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{n-1})$ are sent to $\bd{0}$ and since $(\T-\lambda_n\I)\vecv_n\in\Span(\{\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{n-1}\})$, $\vecv_n$ also gets sent to $\bd{0}$. This map sends all basis vectors to $\bd{0}$ so it is the zero operator.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation such that it can be written as an upper triangular matrix. Then the eigenvalues of $\T$ are the diagonal entries of said matrix.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $(\bd{v}_1,\dots,\bd{v}_n)$ be a basis by which $\T$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Clearly, $\T(\bd{v}_1)=\lambda_1\bd{v}_1$. We again use the fact that $(\T-\lambda_k\I)\bd{v}_k\in\Span(\{\bd{v}_1,\dots,\bd{v}_{k-1}\})$. Notice that $\T-\lambda_k\I:\Span(\{\bd{v}_1,\dots,\bd{v}_k\})\to\Span(\{\bd{v}_1,\dots,\bd{v}_{k-1}\})$. In particular, this map is not injective and its kernel is nontrivial. Let $\bd{v}\in\ker(\T-\lambda_k\I)$ with $\bd{v}\neq\bd{0}$.
    \begin{gather*}
        (\T-\lambda_k\I)\bd{v}=\bd{0}\\
        \T(\bd{v})-\lambda_k\bd{v}=\bd{0}\\
        \T(\bd{v})=\lambda_k\bd{v}
    \end{gather*}
    This shows $\lambda_k$ is an eigenvalue of $\T$. Thus, all entries on the diagonal are eigenvalues. It remains to show all eigenvalues are also on the diagonal. Consider $q(x)=(x-\lambda_1)\dots(x-\lambda_n)$. By the previous Lemma,
    $$q(\T)=(\T-\lambda_1\I)\dots(\T-\lambda_n\I)=0$$
    Note that the minimal polynomial of $\T$ must divide $q$. We know that the roots of the minimal polynomial are exactly all the eigenvalues of $\T$ so they are also roots of $q$. These are on the diagonal of $\T$ because the roots of $q$ is exactly $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$.
\end{proof}
We now give a characterization of what types of linear transformations can be written as upper triangular matrices.
\begin{theorem}
    Let $V$ be a finite dimensional vector space and $\T:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Then $\T$ has an upper triangular matrix with entries $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ if and only if the minimal polynomial of $\T$ splits into linear factors.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

\textbf{Step 1:}

    Assume $\T$ can be written as a upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Then by Lemma 6.1.6, we can write
    \[
    (\T-\lambda_1\I)\dots(\T-\lambda_n\I)=\bd{0}
    \]
    So the polynomial
    \[
    q(x)=(x-\lambda_1)\dots(x-\lambda_n)
    \]
    annihilates $\T$. Then Corollary 6.1.5, tells us that the minimal polynomial of $\T$ divides $q$. Since $q$ only has linear factors, so does $p$.

\textbf{Step 2:}

    Assume that we can write the minimal polynomial of $\T$ as
    \[
    p(x)=(x-\mu_1)\dots(x-\mu_k)
    \]
    Define
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $p_0(x)=1$
        \item $p_1(x)=(x-\mu_1)$
        \item $p_2(x)=(x-\mu_2)(x-\mu_1)$
        \item $\vdots$
        \item $p_k(x)=(x-\mu_k)p_{k-1}(x)=p(x)$
    \end{enumerate}
    Define $W_i:=\ker(p_i(\T))$ and observe that
    \[
    \{\bd{0}\}=W_0\subseteq W_1\subseteq\dots\subseteq W_k=V
    \]
    Construct a basis $(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_n)$ by first taking a basis for $W_1$ then extending it to a basis of $W_2$ and continuing until you have a basis for $W_k=V$. We claim that in this basis, $\T$ is upper triangular.

    First, we observe that for any $\vecv\in V$, we have that
    \[
    \bd{0}=p_i(\T)\vecv=p_{i-1}(x)(\T-\mu_i\I)\vecv
    \]
    So $(\T-\mu_i\I)\vecv\in W_{i-1}$. Now take any $\vecv_j$ in the basis. It was added to the basis for some $W_j$ and from the observation this means that it belongs to $(\T-\mu_j\I)\vecv_j\in W_{j-1}$. But a basis for $W_{j-1}$ is $(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{j-1})$ so $\vecv_j\in\Span(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{j-1})$ and $\T$ is upper triangular.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Schur's Theorem]
    Let $V$ be a complex vector space. Then any linear transformation $T:V\to V$ has an associated upper triangular matrix.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Let $p$ be the minimal polynomial of $\T$. Since we are over the field $\C$, $p$ factors completely into linear terms. Thus, by Theorem 6.1.8, we can write $\T$ in an upper triangular form.
\end{proof}

\section{Generalized Eigenspaces}

Here we will build towards the theory of a useful matrix form called the Jordan Normal Form. Along the way, we will discover another interpretation of algebraic multiplicity. Currently, we know algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue as its degree in the characteristic polynomial, but we have seen little to no significance in using it at all. The most important part of Generalized Eigenspaces is that they decompose the vector space i.e. we can write $V=\bigoplus_i G_{\lambda_i}$. Often times we want to analyze how an operator acts on $V$ but this is a huge task for a large vector space. However, this task becomes much easier when we look at the smaller spaces that we can decompose $V$ into.

\begin{definition}
    Let $V$ be a finite dimensional complex vector space and $\bd{A}:V\to V$ be a linear map. Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $\bd{A}$. We say that $\bd{v}\in V$ is a generalized eigenvalue of $\bd{A}$ corresponding to $\lambda$ if there exists $k\in\N$ such that $(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k\bd{v}=\bd{0}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    We define the generalized eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda$ to be
    \[
    G_\lambda :=\bigcup_{k\in\N}\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k
    \]
\end{definition}

The eigenspace $E_{\lambda}$ is essentially all vectors that are one step away from $\bd{0}$ under $\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I}$. However, the generalized eigenspace $G_{\lambda}$ are all vectors that can eventually hit zero under some finite number of steps. The above definition is inpractical as it is an infinite union however we can simplify it. First, observe that this is an increasing sequence of kernels so
\[
\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k\subseteq\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^{k+1}
\]
Next, we observe that if there exists $k\in\N$ such that
\[
\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k=\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^{k+1}
\]
then for all $j\geq k$ we have
\[
\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k=\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^{j}
\]
So if there exists some point at which the kernels are no longer growing then we have permanently stopped growing. Lastly, $\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\bd{I})^k\subseteq V$ and $V$ has finite dimension so these kernels can only grow so much. An increasing sequence that is bounded from above must stop changing at some point hence the infinite union defining $G_{\lambda}$ is equal to $\ker(\A-\lambda\I)^k$ for some value $k$.

We will now show that the generalized eigenspaces decompose the space. To begin, we will use two Lemmas.

\begin{lemma}[Bezout's Identity for Polynomials]
    Let $p$ and $q$ be polynomials over some field $\mathbb{F}$ and $d=\gcd(p,q)$. Then there exists some polynomials $a$ and $b$ over $\mathbb{F}$ such that $d(x)=a(x)p(x)+b(x)q(x)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    See an Abstract Algebra class.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
    Let $V$ be a finite dimensional vector space and $\bd{A}:V\to V$ be a linear map with $\lambda$ as an eigenvalue. Then
    \[
        V=G_{\lambda}\oplus \im(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{d}
    \]
    where $G_{\lambda}=\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{d}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By Rank-Nullity,
    \[
        \dim(G_{\lambda})+\dim(\im(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d)=\dim(V)
    \]
    Since the dimensions sum to the entire space, it remains to show that $G_{\lambda}$ and $\im(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d$ have trivial intersection.

    Let $\vecv\in G_{\lambda}\cap \im(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d$. Then we can find $\vecu\in V$ such that $(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d\vecu=\vecv$. Applying $(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d$ to both sides we see $(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{2d}\vecu=\bd{0}$. In particular, $\vecu\in\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{2d}$. However, we know that the kernel stabilized at $d$ so $\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{2d}=\ker(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^{d}$ so $(\bd{A}-\lambda\I)^d\vecu=\vecv=\bd{0}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $V$ be a finite dimensional complex vector space and $\bd{A}:V\to V$ be a linear map. Let the eigenvalues of $\bd{A}$ be $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Then the following are true:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item For $i,j\in[n]$ with $i\neq j$, $G_{\lambda_i}\cap G_{\lambda_j}=\{\bd{0}\}$
        \item $V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^n G_{\lambda_i}$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    \textbf{Step 1}:

    Let $\bd{v}\in G_{\lambda_i}\cap G_{\lambda_j}$ and $d_i,d_j\in\N$ be such that $(\bd{A}-\lambda_i\bd{I})^{d_i}\bd{v}=(\bd{A}-\lambda_j\bd{I})^{d_j}\bd{v}=\bd{0}$. Define $p(x)=(x-\lambda_i)^{d_i}$ and $q(x)=(x-\lambda_j)^{d_j}$. Since $\lambda_i\neq\lambda_j$, $\gcd(p,q)=1$. By Bezout's Identity, we can find polynomials $a$ and $b$ such that
    \[
    a(x)p(x)+b(x)q(x)=1
    \]
    Plugging in $\bd{A}$ and evaluating at $\bd{v}$, we get
    \[
    \bd{v}=\I\bd{v}=a(\bd{A})p(\bd{A})\vecv+b(\bd{A})q(\bd{A})\vecv=a(\bd{A})\bd{0}+b(\bd{A})\bd{0}=\bd{0}
    \]

    \textbf{Step 2:}

    We proceed by induction on $n$ (the number of eigenvalues). Let $n=1$. We claim that $\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}=\{\bd{0}\}$. Suppose the space is nonzero. Then we can restrict $\bd{A}\restriction_{\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}}:\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}\to \im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}$. Since it is over the complex numbers, this map must have at least one eigenvector $\vecw$. But then $\vecw\in G_{\lambda_1}\cap\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}$ so $\vecw=\bd{0}$ but eigenvectors can't be the zero vector. Thus, $\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}$ must be trivial and $V=G_{\lambda_1}$.

    Let $n\in\N$ and assume the statement holds for all smaller dimensions. Again we consider the restriction $\bd{A}\restriction_{\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}}:\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}\to \im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}$. Note that $\bd{A}\restriction_{\im(\bd{A}-\lambda_1\I)^{d_1}}$ is injective and has eigenvalues $\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_{n-1}$. Thus, we can apply the Induction Hypothesis to get that
    \[
    V=G_{\lambda_1}\oplus\left(\bigoplus_{i=2}^nG_{\lambda_i}\right)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^nG_{\lambda_i}
    \]
\end{proof}
Now we build towards the Jordan Normal Form of a matrix. This form is particularly useful for computing matrix exponentials i.e. $e^{\A}:=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\A^k}{k!}$ because it turns this infinite summation into a finite one (this will be an exercise). Matrix exponentials are extremely useful in many areas of math particularly differential equations.
\begin{definition}
    A block diagonal matrix $\A$ is a matrix of the form
    \[
    \A=\begin{bmatrix}
        \A_1 & & & \\
        & \A_2 & & \\
        & & \ddots & \\
        & & & \A_{k-1} & \\
        & & & & \A_k
    \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    where $\A_1,\dots,\A_k$ are square matrices along the main diagonal and all other entries are $0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $V$ be a complex vector space and $\A:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Then there exists a basis $B$ such that $\A$ is block diagonal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\A$ have distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots\lambda_k$. Consider $\A\restriction_{G_{\lambda_1}}$. We know that $\A(G_{\lambda_1})\subseteq G_{\lambda_1}$. Let $B_1$ be a basis for $G_{\lambda_1}$. This means that when we apply $\A\restriction_{G_{\lambda_1}}$ to $G_{\lambda_1}$ we only need basis vectors from $B_1$ to represent the result. Let $\A_1$ be a $\dim(G_{\lambda_1})\times\dim(G_{\lambda_1})$ matrix representing $\A\restriction_{G_{\lambda_1}}$ from $B_1$ to $B_1$. Define $\A_2,\dots,\A_k$ similarly. Then we have for $B=(B_1,\dots,B_k)$
    \[
    [\A]_{BB}=\begin{bmatrix}
        \A_1 & & & \\
        & \A_2 & & \\
        & & \ddots & \\
        & & & \A_{k-1} & \\
        & & & & \A_k
    \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    First, we note that $B$ is a basis for $V$ since We know that $V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^k G_{\lambda_i}$. Within each generalized eigenspace, the image of that generalized eigenspace is contained with itself. Thus, we only need the vectors from that individual space to represent the output. This leads to the blocks for each generalized eigenspace.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Let $\bd{N}$ be a linear transformation. We say that $\bd{N}$ is nilpotent if there exists $k\in\N$ such that $\bd{N}^k=\bd{0}$
\end{definition}
Suppose we are working in a generalized eigenspace $G_{\lambda}$ for a linear transformation $\T$. We know that $(\T-\lambda\I)^k=\bd{0}$ for some $k\in\N$ inside $G_{\lambda}$. In particular, $\bd{N}:=\T-\lambda\I$ is nilpotent. Using this we can write, $\T=\bd{N}+\lambda\I$ so if we can understand the nilpotent matrix $\bd{N}$ then all we have to do is add $\lambda$ on the diagonal to understand $\T$.

Further suppose that you can find some $\vecv$ such that for some constant $j\in\N$, $\bd{N}^j\vecv=\bd{0}$ but $\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv\neq\bd{0}$. We call the sequence $\vecv,\bd{N}\vecv,\bd{N}^2\vecv,\dots,\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv$ a Jordan Chain. Now let $\vecv_1=\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv,\vecv_2=\bd{N}^{j-2}\vecv,\dots$. We observe that
\begin{gather*}
    \bd{N}\vecv_1=\bd{0}\\
    \bd{N}\vecv_2=\vecv_1\\
    \bd{N}\vecv_3=\vecv_2\\
    \vdots\\
    \bd{N}\vecv_j=\vecv_{j-1}
\end{gather*}
We will be particularly concerned with cycles of this form.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\bd{N}$ be a nilpotent matrix and $\vecv,\bd{N}\vecv,\bd{N}^2\vecv,\dots,\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv$ be a Jordan Chain. Then $\vecv,\bd{N}\vecv,\bd{N}^2\vecv,\dots,\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv$ are linearly independent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Suppose there exists constants $\alpha_0,\dots\alpha_{j-1}$ such that
    \[\alpha_0\vecv+\alpha_1\bd{N}\vecv+\alpha_2\bd{N}^2\vecv+\dots+\alpha_{j-1}\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv=\bd{0}
    \]
    Assume, for sake of contradiction, that there exists some constant that is nonzero. Let $i$ be the smallest index such that $\alpha_i\neq 0$. So all the terms before $\alpha_i$ vanish. Apply $\bd{N}^{j-i-1}$ to both sides to get
    \[
    \alpha_{i}\bd{N}^{j-1}\vecv=\bd{0}
    \]
    since this makes all the terms after $\alpha_i$ vanish. But this implies that $\alpha_i=0$ which is a contradiction.
\end{proof}
Suppose that for a nilpotent matrix $\bd{N}$ we have a Jordan Chain with length equal to the dimension of the space $\vecv, \bd{N}\vecv,\dots,\bd{N}^{n-1}\vecv$. By the previous Theorem, this list is linearly indenpendent and since it has the correct size,  the Jordan Chain is actually a basis for the space. As before, we will name these vectors using the convention $\vecv_i=\bd{N}^{n-i}\vecv$ and define the ordered basis $B=(\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_{n})$. Now we can write $\bd{N}$ in this basis as
\[
[\bd{N}]_{BB}=\begin{bmatrix}
     0 & 1 & & & 0\\
     & 0 & 1 & & \\
     & & \ddots& \ddots & \\
     & & & 0 & 1 \\
     0 & & & & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\]
where the $1$'s are on the diagonal directly above the main diagonal and all other entries are $0$. We will refer to this as a "one-off" matrix.
\begin{definition}
    Let $\bd{N}$ be a nilpotent operator. We say that non-zero vectors $\vecv_1,\dots,\vecv_j$ form a cycle of generalized eigenvectors if $\bd{N}\vecv_1=\bd{0}$ and $\bd{N}\vecv_i=\vecv_{i-1}$ for $i\in[j]$.
\end{definition}
Note that this is essentially just taking a Jordan Chain and reversing it as we did previously. Recall the goal of this section: we want to analyze an operator by understanding how it acts on different pieces of the vector space. These cycles will more or less be the individual pieces that we are looking at.
\begin{theorem}
    Let $\bd{N}:V\to V$ be a nilpotent matrix and $\mathcal{C}_1,\dots,\mathcal{C}_k$ be cycles of generalized eigenvectors where $\mathcal{C}_i=\vecv_1^i,\dots,\vecv_{\ell_i}^i$ and the collection of $\vecv^i_1$ is linearly independent. Then $\mathcal{C}_1,\dots,\mathcal{C}_k$ are pairwise disjoint and their union is linearly independent.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Without loss of generality, we assume that the $\vecv_j^i$'s span $V$. If not, we instead consider the restriction of $\bd{N}$ to the invariant subspace which is the span of the $\vecv_j^i$'s. Let $n=\ell_1+\dots+\ell_k$. We proceed by induction on $n$. If $n=1$, then there is nothing to prove. Now suppose that for all operators and all cycles such that the total number of vectors is strictly less than $n$, the Theorem holds.

    Consider the subspace $\im\bd{N}$. By the definition of a cycle, any vector in the image does not use the vector $\vecv^i_{\ell_i}$. Each vector $\vecv^i_j$ maps to the vector "below" it $\vecv^i_{j-1}$ so the final vector $\vecv^i_{\ell_i}$ is never used. This means that the cycles $\mathcal{C}'_i=\vecv^i_1,\dots,\vecv^i_{\ell_{i}-1}$ form a collection of $n-k$ vectors. Thus, we can apply the Induction Hypothesis to get that all of these vectors are linearly independent. 
    
    Since these vectors also span the image, we get that $\dim(\im\bd{N})=n-k$. Observe that all of the $\vecv_1^i$'s belong to $\ker(\bd{N})$ and that they are linearly independent. This implies that $\dim(\ker(\bd{N}))\geq k$. Rank-Nullity tells us that
    \[
    \dim(V)=\dim(\im(\bd{N}))+\dim(\ker(\bd{N}))\geq n-k+k=n
    \]
    We assumed that the collection of the $n$ $\vecv^i_j$'s spans $V$ so in fact these vectors form a basis for $V$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    It seems like at the end we conclude that in fact the union of any number of cycles forms a basis for the space. This should be extremely suspicious as we can simply consider the case when we only have $1$ cycle and there should be no reason this forms a basis. This is hidden in the fact that we assumed, without loss of generality, that the vectors span the vector space. In general, this means we are forced to restrict to the smaller space which is the true span of the collection of cycles.
\end{remark}

We have shown that unions of "special" cycles are linearly independent. The natural next step now is to go from linear independence to a basis. This next Theorem will show just that and you will notice that the argument is borderline the inverse of the previous argument: instead of removing a vector from each chain, we will take a chain and add a vector.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\bd{N}:V\to V$ be a nilpotent matrix. Then there exists a basis of $V$ which is the union of cycles of generalized eigenvectors of $\bd{N}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $n=\dim(V)$. We proceed by induction on $n$. If $n=1$, then there is nothing to prove. Assume that the Theorem holds for any nilpotent operator on a space of dimension strictly less than $n$.

    Since $\bd{N}$ is nilpotent, $\bd{N}$ is not invertible (exercise). Thus, $\dim(\im\bd{N})<\dim(V)=n$. Furthermore, $\bd{N}$ is invariant on $\im\bd{N}$ so consider the operator that is $\bd{N}_{\restriction_{\im\bd{N}}}:\im\bd{N}\to \im\bd{N}$. We can apply the Induction Hypothesis to $\bd{N}_{\restriction_{\im\bd{N}}}$ to get a collection of cycles $\mathcal{C}_1,\dots,\mathcal{C}_k$ where $\mathcal{C}_i=\vecv^i_1,\dots,\vecv^i_{\ell_i}$ and their union forms a basis for $\im\bd{N}$.

    Since $\vecv^i_{\ell_i}\in\im\bd{N}$, we can find a vector $\vecv^i_{\ell_i+1}$ such that $\bd{N}\vecv^i_{\ell_i+1}=\vecv^i_{\ell_i}$. Now consider the cycles $\mathcal{C}_i'=\vecv^i_1,\dots,\vecv^i_{\ell_i},\vecv^i_{\ell_i+1}$. We know the collection of $\mathcal{C}_1,\dots,\mathcal{C}_k$ form a basis, so the $\vecv_1^i$'s are linearly independent. This means that the previous Theorem tells us that the union of the $\mathcal{C}'_i$'s is linearly independent.

    The vectors $\vecv^i_1$ are linearly independent and all belong to $\ker(\bd{N})$, so we can extend them to a basis of $\ker(\bd{N})$, $\vecv^1_1,\dots\vecv^k_1,\vecu_1,\dots\vecu_j$. Treating the $\vecu_h$'s as cycles of length $1$, the previous Theorem tells us that the union $\mathcal{C}_1',\dots,\mathcal{C}_k',\vecu_1,\dots,\vecu_j$ is linearly independent. We claim that this union is a basis for $V$.

    Observe that the total number of vectors in $\mathcal{C}'_1,\dots,\mathcal{C}'_k$ is $\dim(\im\bd{N})+k$ since we constructed these cycles by taking a basis for the image and then adding one vector to each cycle in the basis for the image. We also know that the dimension of the kernel is $k+j$.

    This means that the total number of vectors in $\mathcal{C}_1',\dots,\mathcal{C}_k',\vecu_1,\dots,\vecu_j$ is $$\dim(\im(\bd{N}))+k+j=\dim(\im(\bd{N}))+\dim(\ker(\bd{N}))=\dim(V)$$
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
    Let $\bd{N}:V\to V$ be a nilpotent matrix. A basis of $V$ that can be written as the union of cycles of generalized eigenvectors of $\bd{N}$ is called a Jordan Canonical Basis for $\bd{N}$.
\end{definition}

Now we can define the Jordan Canonical Form of a nilpotent matrix.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $\bd{N}:V\to V$ be a nilpotent matrix. Then there exists a basis $B$ of $V$ such that $[\bd{N}]_{BB}$ is block diagonal, $[\bd{N}]_{BB}=\mathrm{diag}(\A_1,\dots,\A_k)$ where $\A_i$ are all "one-off" matrices except for at most one which can be a zero matrix.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We know that we can find a Jordan Canonical Basis $B$. A length $\ell$ cycle corresponds to a $\ell\times\ell$ "one-off" matrix. When $\ell=1$, this is just a $1\times 1$ matrix containing $0$. To get the at most one zero matrix, we simply place these $1\times 1$ matrices next to each other so if we have $k$ of them they form a $k\times k$ zero matrix since all other entries are zero and all the diagonal entries are zero.
\end{proof}

Now we return to general matrices $\bd{A}:V\to V$ and show that we can decompose them.

\begin{theorem}[Jordan Decomposition Theorem]
    Let $\bd{A}:V\to V$ be a linear transformation. Then there exists a basis $B$ of $V$ such that $[\A]_{BB}$ is block diagonal and each block has the form
    \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \lambda & 1 & & &\\
        & \lambda & 1 & &\\
        & & \ddots & \ddots &\\
        & & & \lambda & 1\\
        & & & & \lambda
    \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    where $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\A$. This form is known as the Jordan Canonical Form (or Jordan Normal Form) of $\A$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ be the distinct eigenvalues of $\A$. We know that we can find a basis where $\A$ is in block diagonal form with blocks $\A_1,\dots,\A_n$ ($\A_k=\A\restriction_{G_{\lambda_k}}$) corresponding to each individual generalized eigenspace. Define the nilpotent operator $\bd{N}_k=\bd{A}_k-\lambda_k\I$ and choose a basis of $G_{\lambda_k}$ where $\bd{N}_k$ is in Jordan Canonical Form. Then adding $\lambda_k\I$ yields the desired form.
\end{proof}

    \end{enumerate}

\begin{exercise}

    \[
    e^{\A}:=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{\A^k}{k!}
    \]
    Note that this is just the Taylor Series for $e^x$ with $\A$ plugged in. Unfortunately, this is an infinite sum and summing infinite powers of matrices is generally impossible, but Jordan Normal Form makes this much better.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item Show that for $n\times n$ matrices $\A,\bd{B}$ such that $\A$ and $\bd{B}$ commute, $e^{\A+\B}=e^{\A}e^{\B}$. Note that this is false in general without commutativity.
        \item Find an alternate formula for $e^{\A}$ with a finite summation. (Hint: Use Jordan Normal Form!)
    \end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}
